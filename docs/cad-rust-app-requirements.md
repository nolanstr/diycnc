# DIYCNC on cad-rust-app: what it would need

## Status (2026-10-28)

DIYCNC now runs on cad-rust-app instead of FreeCAD. The changes are on a local branch, `diycnc`, of
cad-rust-app in `~/dev/cad-rust-app`:

- **Python bindings** (`python/`, built with maturin into the `diycnc` conda env), in three layers:
  - `cadrust._core`: OpenCascade shapes, operations and queries.
  - `cadrust.freecad`: a FreeCAD-style API on them (`Vector`, `Matrix`, `Rotation`, `Placement`, `BoundBox`,
    `Part.makeBox` and the rest), so the model scripts changed only their imports.
  - `cadrust.view`: rendering.
- **New kernel wrappers** in `extra.hxx`:
  - primitives placed on an axis;
  - polygons, segments and three-point arcs;
  - sub-shape maps in OCCT's order, outer and ordered wires;
  - cross-sections joined into wires (`slice`) and shape-to-shape distance;
  - points along edges and wires, and surface parameters;
  - booleans with options, FreeCAD-style lofts and chamfers;
  - STL with linear and angular deflection;
  - STEP with names, colors and assemblies (XCAF);
  - a threaded batch of common volumes for the collision check.
- **A renderer**: parallel, on the CPU, with edge lines, translucency, a transform per part and an
  orthographic camera fitted to a box. The machine renders, the build steps and the labeled mold figures use it.
- **FEA**, in the app's `fea_mesh.rs` and `fea_solve.rs` (so the Simulation tab can use it too):
  - each part meshed at its own element size (`mesh_sized`);
  - parts tied where they touch (`ties`: a surface node of the finer part follows the coarser part's face);
  - springs between patches or to the ground, and lumped masses;
  - many load cases on one factorization.

  From Python it is `cadrust._core.FeModel`, with patches, supports, springs and masses, solves, average
  displacements, modes and each part's share of a mode's energy. Against beam theory it is within 1 %, tied
  or not.
- **A `set_parts` tool** for the app's scripts, and **a native document**: `CNC_DOC=1` writes
  `model/DIYCNC.json`, with a Part Studio per container, slider and revolute mates limited to the travel, and
  the frame's explode steps.

The results match FreeCAD's:
- `report.txt` and `steel.json` (every hole) are identical.
- The 79 print STLs agree in volume to under 0.001 %.
- The voxels agree to a few boundary sub-samples, and the frame solve gives the same 98.3 / 153.1 / 131.3
  N/µm.
- Two mold boards place a pair of screw holes in the mirror-image spot, a float-noise tie that passes the
  same checks.
- A build takes about 60 s (FreeCAD: 72 s). With the STEP, the document and the 24 renders it takes about
  2 minutes, and the 32 mold figures take 40 s. None of it needs a window.

Still open from the lists below:
- **A FluidNC post.** The GRBL post works with FluidNC, but it assumes the controller can't keep a
  tool-setter measurement.
- **A native `volume_fractions`.** Slicing is fast enough.
- **The whole frame as tetrahedra.** The 3 mm tube walls need elements about that small, which puts the frame
  near 5 million unknowns, beyond 16 GB. `analysis/tet_check.py` instead compares one upright, as
  tetrahedra and as voxels. Two tet meshes agree within 2 %, and the voxels close in on them: 8-11 % too
  stiff at 10 mm, within 2 % at 7.5 and 5 mm. So the 5 mm voxel figures for the frame are the ones to
  believe.
- The Python bindings expose the operations DIYCNC uses, not the whole kernel.

## Background

DIYCNC is a desktop CNC mill for cutting steel. Its frame is five epoxy-granite castings with skeletons of
stock steel flat bar and tube cast inside them: a two-piece base, two uprights and a beam. It has
180 × 300 × 140 mm of travel, HGR15 rails and ballscrews on all three axes, and a FluidNC controller.

Everything is generated by one parametric Python script, `model/build_cnc.py`, with its helper modules
`parts.py`, `molds.py`, `moldbox.py` and `mold_figures.py`, about 6,200 lines in all. The script builds
the machine, every cast-in steel part with its drilled and tapped holes, and the molds with their
3D-printed pieces. It then checks for collisions at 18 axis positions, and writes:

- STEP and STL files;
- a FreeCAD document;
- the PNG renders;
- a report and the Markdown for the build guides, cut lists and drilling templates.

A separate voxel finite-element model (`analysis/`, Python with numpy, scipy and CHOLMOD) studies the
frame's stiffness and vibration (about 2,500 lines). It only uses FreeCAD to turn the geometry into voxels.

It runs headless in FreeCAD 1.1 (OpenCascade 7.8). The renders and the mold illustrations need the FreeCAD
window.

This is a companion to the ZeroTorque requirements (`cad-rust-app-requirements.md` in that repo). Part 2
of that document lists most of the kernel features this project needs as well:
- a headless, scriptable interface that returns values;
- multi-argument booleans and remove-splitter;
- fillets on edges chosen by geometry, solved in one operation;
- volume, bounding box, center of mass and point classification;
- STEP and STL export, PNG renders, and a document with a parameter table.

This document lists only what DIYCNC needs on top of that, and what it can do without.

As with ZeroTorque, we would use cad-rust-app in two ways, in this order:

1. **CAM and FEA on our exported parts.** This needs few changes on your side.
2. **Running the generator itself in the app**, replacing FreeCAD.

## Part 1: CAM and FEA on our STEP files

### CAM: the machine making its own parts

Once the mill runs, its first jobs are its own upgrades and accessories: vise jaws, fixtures, brackets,
and the mounts for a 4th and later a 5th axis. These are mostly 6061 aluminum, with some mild steel.

- **Post:** FluidNC. It takes GRBL-style G-code, so the GRBL post may already work. Please confirm how
  it handles arcs (G2/G3, including helices), tool changes (manual, M6 with a pause) and spindle
  speed (S, 0–24,000 rpm, from a 0–10 V VFD).
- **Machine preset:**
  - travel X 180, Y 300, Z 140 mm;
  - a manual tool change, with an automatic changer to come later;
  - an ER20 collet (ER11 or ER16 with the 1.5 kW spindle);
  - flood coolant.
- **Workholding:** the 6061 table is the interface for every module: an M6 grid at 25 mm pitch, with two 8 mm
  dowels on the X center line that a vise plate, a 4th-axis plate or a trunnion base lands on. Fixtures
  defined on that grid would be useful.
- **Operations:** the 2.5D set, drilling and tapping, and adaptive clearing in steel at light depths of
  cut. The spindle is 1.5–2.2 kW; the frame is stiff, but the spindle and Z axis are the limit.

### FEA: an independent check on our voxel model

This is the most useful thing the app could do for us now. Our voxel model uses 8-node bricks with each
voxel's modulus mixed from its steel and epoxy-granite fractions. It doesn't converge well:

| Voxel size | Frame stiffness X / Y / Z (N/µm) |
|---|---|
| 10 mm | 98.7 / 154.4 / 133.4 |
| 7.5 mm | 95.4 / 151.0 / 125.5 |
| 5 mm | 85.5 / 132.5 / 113.6 |

The most likely cause is the thin steel (3.2 mm tube walls, 9.5 mm bars) smeared over the voxels.
10-node tetrahedra that model the steel as its own bodies would show where the real answer is.

What the study needs:

- **Many bodies from one STEP file, bonded together, each with its own material:**
  - the five castings (epoxy granite: E = 30 GPa, ν = 0.25, ρ = 2.35 g/cm³);
  - about 110 steel parts cast inside them (E = 200 GPa, ν = 0.25 as in our model, ρ = 7.85 g/cm³): the
    58 pieces of the skeletons, plus pads, nuts and studs.
  
  The steel is fully embedded, so the contact is bonded. The joints between castings are clamped by tie
  rods, and bonded is fine there too.
- **Element size per body.** A 3 mm tube wall sits inside a 600 mm casting, so a single global size gives
  either far too many elements or too few through the walls.
- **Loads brought in over a patch (RBE3-style).** The 1 kN cutting force goes in at the tool and comes out
  at the work. On the frame, it goes through the four rail-block footprints on each axis and the ballscrew's
  fixed bearing. The load cases are self-equilibrated, so the feet carry nothing.
- **Many load cases in one solve:** 1 kN in X, Y and Z, at 2 tool positions and 3 table positions, which
  is 18 cases.
- **Springs:** each rail block and the ballscrews have a catalog stiffness (an HG15C block at light
  preload is 196 N/µm). This is needed to carry the analysis on through the moving parts to the tool.
- **Modal analysis with lumped masses:**
  - the head (22.2 kg) and the table (12 kg) tied rigidly to their rail blocks;
  - the frame standing on four feet, held in Z at all four and in-plane at the front pair.
  
  We find the in-plane supports change the first mode by ±10 %, so being able to choose them matters.
- **Results as numbers:** the displacement of the tool point relative to the work point for each load case,
  and the first six frequencies with each body's share of the kinetic energy. A colored picture is not
  enough.

## Part 2: running the generator in the app

### 2.1 Python, not Rhai

This is the main requirement, and the main difference from ZeroTorque.

- The generator is about 6,200 lines of Python, and the analysis is about 2,500 more with numpy and scipy.
  Rewriting it in Rhai isn't realistic.
- The Rhai custom features (boxes, cylinders, prisms, move, rotate, mirror, fillet-all, booleans, volume)
  and the JSON tool calls (`cad-app run`, MCP) can't return geometry to the script. A script can't ask for
  an edge's vertices, a face's normal or a bounding box and decide what to do next.
- What would work: Python bindings (PyO3) to the kernel wrapper (`occ.rs` and the vendored `opencascade`
  crate). Shapes would be Python objects with the operations and queries below, and the document would be
  writable from Python.

### 2.2 Geometry operations, per build

Measured with cProfile over one full build of the current model, which takes 72 s:

| Call | Count | Time |
|---|---|---|
| common | 2,224 | 32 s |
| cut | 1,157 | 19 s |
| multi-fuse (several tools in one operation) | 299 | 10.5 s |
| fuse | 297 | 1.8 s |
| point classification (`isInside`) | 2,902 | 1.0 s |
| remove splitter | 181 | 1.0 s |
| fillet, on chosen edges | 126 | 0.3 s |
| chamfer, on chosen edges | 26 | 0.2 s |
| extrude a face | 1,327 | |
| shape-to-shape distance (`distToShape`) | 87 | |
| loft | 8 | |
| tessellate to a mesh, absolute deflection | 79 | 0.8 s |
| cylinders / boxes / polygons / spheres / cones | 1,816 / 523 / 1,355 / 62 / 38 | |

Like ZeroTorque, nearly all the time is in booleans.

The fillets pick their edges by vertex position more than by face normals. Typical picks:
- "edges parallel to X whose vertices all lie on z = c";
- "edges whose vertices all have y greater than the beam's back face minus 1 mm".

Each fillet then tries a list of radii and keeps the first one OpenCascade accepts. So the edge queries
need an edge's vertices and direction, and a failed fillet must come back as an error the script can catch.

### 2.3 Queries the analysis needs

- **Cross-sections:** cut a solid with a plane, and get back closed wires that can be discretized into
  polygons. `analysis/voxelize.py` slices every structural part (about 120 solids) at 4 sample heights per
  voxel layer: about 400 heights at 5 mm voxels. It then fills the polygons to get each voxel's volume
  fraction.

  Better still would be a built-in `volume_fractions(shape, origin, voxel_size, counts, subsamples)` that
  returns an array. At 5 mm that is 1.7 million voxels × 64 samples, so it needs to be native and parallel.
- **Interference:** the shared volume between pairs of parts, for the collision check. Bounding boxes cull
  the pairs first (481,984 box tests per build), and the rest are `common` calls: 1,868 of the 2,224, and
  27 of the 32 s. A built-in batch interference check over many bodies would cut this the most.
- **Minimum distance between two shapes.**
- Volume, bounding box, validity and solid count, as in the ZeroTorque list.

### 2.4 The document

- **Hundreds of named bodies in nested groups:** castings, cast-in steel, joint hardware, rails,
  ballscrews, motors, the enclosure, and a hidden group of mold pieces. Each body has a "look" (material
  and color) that the renders and the bill of materials use.
- **Variables that move bodies.** The FreeCAD document has a `Params` table:
  - `posX`, `posY`, `posZ` jog the axes;
  - `doorAngle` opens the enclosure door;
  - `explode` pulls the castings apart;
  - `sumpOut` slides out the coolant sump.
  
  Each drives the placement of a whole group of bodies through an expression. In the app, variables drive
  sketch dimensions. We would need either body transforms bound to expressions, or assembly slider and
  revolute mates whose offset is a variable.

### 2.5 Rendering

The app's `render` takes a named view or a direction and a size. We make 11 views of the machine, 13 build
steps and 32 mold illustrations, which need:

- **An arbitrary camera:** an orientation matrix or position, target and up, orthographic. The images
  are 2800 × 2100 on a white background.
- **Per-body color and transparency.** The x-ray views draw the castings at 75 % transparency, so the
  steel inside shows.
- **A subset of bodies per image.** Each build step shows what has been assembled so far.
- **Section cutaways** of chosen bodies.
- **Labeled callouts** (leader lines and text placed in 3D) for the mold illustrations, now done in
  `mold_figures.py` in the FreeCAD window.
- Animations aren't needed: we make those from our own FEA results with PyVista.

### 2.6 Output

- **STL per body at an absolute linear and angular deflection** (0.05 mm, 0.25 rad): 79 files for the
  3D-printed parts. These feed Bambu Studio's command-line slicer.
- **One STEP of the whole machine**, with body names and colors and the group structure.
- A native document that can be opened to jog the axes (2.4).

### 2.7 Performance

A build takes 72 s. The renders add a few minutes, and the voxels for the analysis take several minutes
more. The ZeroTorque points on parallel booleans, multi-tool fuses and caching by shape identity apply here
too. The collision check is the biggest single cost (2.2): 18 axis positions, each body placed and tested
against every other that could touch it.

## What DIYCNC doesn't need from the ZeroTorque list

- **The heavy 2D offsetting.** It is used 3 times per build.
- **The ray-cast machinability check, the combinatorial weight search and the balance solver.**
- **Mass properties beyond volume and center of mass.** The inertia tensor is used only for the
  lumped masses in our own modal analysis.

## Acceptance tests

**Part 1 (CAM and FEA):**
- Import `DIYCNC.step`, which the model writes when it runs in the FreeCAD window. Mesh the five castings and the steel
  inside them as bonded bodies, each with its own material.
- Run the three 1 kN load cases at the center position, and report the tool-to-work stiffness in X, Y and Z.
- Run the modal analysis with the 22.2 kg head and 12 kg table, and report the first four frequencies.
- We expect these below our 5 mm voxel figures (85.5 / 132.5 / 113.6 N/µm; first modes about 130–150 Hz),
  because the bricks are stiff. A result near them would also tell us the voxel model is good enough.
- Post a FluidNC program with helical arcs and a tool change, and check that it runs in FluidNC's
  simulator.

**Part 2 (generator):**
- The build reproduces `docs/5-design/report.txt` (dimensions, casting volumes to 0.01 L, steel mass to
  0.1 kg, "No collisions") and `docs/src/boards/steel.json` (every hole) exactly.
- The 79 print STLs match ours in volume to 0.1 %.
- The base casting's fillets, chosen by vertex position, succeed with the same radii as FreeCAD's.
- `volume_fractions` on the frame matches `voxelize.py` to 1e-3 per voxel at 10 mm.
- A full build takes no longer than FreeCAD's 72 s.
